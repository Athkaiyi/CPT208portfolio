SyncValley A Cozy Web App for Fairer Group Collaboration

SyncValley is a cozy, AI-supported group collaboration web app designed to reduce free-riding in student projects. This project supports real group work through staged task planning, AI guidance, AI evaluation, visible contribution, coin rewards, and a gentle forest-growth feedback system.

02 · MOTIVATION

Why We Chose the Social Track

We chose the social track because unfair participation in student group projects is one of the most common and emotionally draining problems in university life. In many teams, a small number of students take responsibility for planning, coordination, and delivery, while others contribute less consistently or disengage until the final deadline. This creates frustration, weakens trust, and turns collaboration into a source of stress rather than shared learning.

Through reflection and feedback, we recognised that the issue is not only about monitoring contribution. Some students become passive because they do not receive timely positive reinforcement, while others avoid participation because they do not know how to begin or how to complete their assigned work. A meaningful solution therefore needs to support fairness, motivation, and task clarity at the same time.

SyncValley responds to this challenge by embedding support directly into the group workflow. It helps teams divide work into stages, limits each member to one task per stage to reduce task hoarding, uses AI to guide students through their work, evaluates submissions more consistently, and translates contribution into gentle visual growth through coins and a personal or shared forest. Rather than punishing low participation, it aims to make progress visible, manageable, and emotionally supportive.

03 · GAP DISCOVERY

What Existing Research and Products Still Miss

To identify design opportunities for SyncValley, we reviewed both academic work on group collaboration and existing digital productivity tools. Across both areas, we found strong support for planning, communication, and contribution tracking. However, we also found that many systems focus more on management than on the emotional and motivational realities of student teamwork.

This review helped us shift the project toward a more embedded collaboration tool. We realised students do not need a separate game layered on top of project work. They need a system that fits naturally into their workflow while still offering encouragement, clarity, and fairness.

Combined Findings from Academic Papers & Commercial Products

Finding 1 — Coordination and visibility

  • Did well: Commercial tools like Trello and Asana clearly support task division, deadlines, and shared progress visibility.
  • Missed: They rarely help users who are unsure how to begin, so visibility does not always turn into action.

Finding 2 — Guidance and scaffolding

  • Did well: Narciss (2008) and van de Pol, Volman & Beishuizen (2010) both state that timely scaffolding and feedback improve task completion.
  • Missed: Although many commercial AI tools such as Microsoft Copilot draft content, they are not tightly tied to team stages or submission flow.

Finding 3 — Motivation and contribution feedback

  • Did well: Academic work on self-determination and gamification, such as Hamari, Koivisto & Sarsa (2014), shows that visible feedback can support motivation.
  • Missed: Products like GitHub contribution graphs can feel too monitoring-like and do not always provide gentle emotional support.
04 · PERSONAS

Primary and Secondary Users

We developed personas to represent two key user groups within student project teams. The first is the reliable contributor who often ends up carrying extra work and becomes frustrated when the team lacks fairness or visibility. The second is the hesitant participant who may be willing to help, but struggles with uncertainty, lack of confidence, or unclear expectations.

06 · REQUIREMENTS

Design Requirements

Based on our research, reflection, and user journey analysis, we defined the following design requirements to support SyncValley as a practical collaboration tool that remains gentle, motivating, and genuinely useful in real student workflows.

R1

Stage-Based Fair Coordination

The system must divide projects into clear stages, lock future stages until current work is finished, and limit each member to one task per stage. This helps prevent free-riding, reduces task hoarding, and makes responsibility more evenly distributed.

R2

AI Support for Starting and Finishing Work

The system must use AI to help students understand what to do, how to do it, and how to prepare evidence for submission. AI guidance should lower the barrier to participation, while AI evaluation should offer structured feedback and fairer reward allocation.

R3

Gentle Positive Feedback

The platform must make contribution visible in a low-pressure way. Instead of punitive warnings or stressful leaderboards, it should use coins, forest growth, and contribution snapshots to encourage participation while maintaining psychological safety.

08 · IDEATION
The Crazy Eights
System Architecture
Design Alternatives

Alternative 1 — Productivity Dashboard

This direction was practical for tracking deadlines and organizing tasks, but it felt too similar to existing project tools and did not respond strongly enough to the emotional and motivational problems we identified.

Alternative 2 — AI Chatbot Assistant

This direction supported task clarification and question answering well, but it focused too narrowly on individual help and was weaker in stage coordination, shared visibility, and contribution transparency across the team.

Alternative 3 — Cozy AI Collaboration Tool

This became the selected direction. It combines staged teamwork, one-task-per-member logic, AI guidance, AI evaluation, coin rewards, and a healing forest metaphor to support fairer and more sustainable participation.

09 · PROTOTYPE

Prototype and Core Flow

The prototype demonstrates the full logic of the revised SyncValley system. Users can move through Home, Tasks, AI, and Garden to understand the current project stage, accept one task per stage, receive AI guidance, submit evidence for AI evaluation, earn coins when their work passes evaluation, and use those coins to grow a personal forest while contributing to a team forest.

The core interaction loop is designed to support real group work rather than simulated play: AI helps teams plan stages, the system structures responsibilities, members receive support while working, evaluation updates contribution fairly, and visual growth provides gentle motivation. This keeps the experience functional while preserving the calm and encouraging tone of the product.

10 · EVALUATION & REFLECTION

Usability Testing and Reflection

We tested the alpha prototype with 3 student participants to evaluate the main interaction loop of SyncValley: understanding the current stage, claiming one task, using AI support, submitting work for evaluation, and receiving contribution feedback through coins and forest growth.

The testing showed that the overall workflow was understandable and that the AI guidance feature was especially valuable for reducing uncertainty. The most important issue was not navigation, but status clarity: some users were unsure when a task was only being guided and when it had officially passed AI evaluation and become completed.

Testing Focus

Instead of measuring success only by whether a participant completed an assigned task, we focused on how participants described the clarity, usefulness, and motivational quality of different parts of the interface. This helped us understand not just whether the workflow was usable, but whether it felt supportive and meaningful to student users.

Participant Feedback on Key Interface Areas
Interface Area Participant Response Main Insight
Home / Stage Overview All 3 participants gave positive feedback about quickly understanding the current stage and team progress. The Home page supported fast orientation and reduced ambiguity at the beginning of collaboration.
Task Claiming Area 2 participants said the one-task-per-stage logic felt fair and easy to understand; 1 wanted clearer reasons for disabled buttons. The claiming structure was seen as fair, but unavailable states needed stronger explanation.
Guide Me / AI Support All 3 participants described this area positively, especially for making vague work feel easier to begin. AI guidance was the most consistently valued feature for reducing uncertainty and task anxiety.
Evaluate / Completion Status Only 2 participants clearly felt the status change was easy to interpret; 1 remained unsure about when a task officially passed. Status communication still needed stronger separation between guidance and formal completion.
Coins, My Forest, and Team Forest All 3 participants responded positively and described the reward system as calm, encouraging, and non-competitive. The visual reward system successfully supported motivation without feeling like a stressful ranking mechanism.
Key Findings

1. Home Supported Fast Orientation

  • Participants could quickly understand the current stage, deadline, and team progress.
  • This suggests the Home page successfully reduces ambiguity at the beginning of collaboration.

2. AI Guidance Reduced Task Anxiety

  • Guide Me was consistently seen as the most useful support feature.
  • It helped transform broad tasks into smaller, more actionable steps, which is especially important for hesitant users.

3. Completion Logic Needed Stronger Signals

  • The biggest issue was the boundary between “being guided” and “officially completed.”
  • This is critical because SyncValley’s fairness model depends on evaluation-based completion and reward allocation.

4. Forest Feedback Worked as Gentle Motivation

  • Participants responded positively to the Garden metaphor and did not interpret it as a stressful ranking system.
  • This supports our decision to visualise contribution through calm growth rather than competitive comparison.

Iterative Refinement

As shown in the comparison below, the earlier version of the TEAM STAGE module was fixed and did not include a vertical side slider. Users could only see the current stage and could not access previously completed stages. At the same time, future stages were placed directly underneath the TEAM STAGE area without a clear Locked label, which made the page structure less clear and weakened the sense of progression across stages.

Based on testing and reflection, we refined the structure to improve hierarchy, visibility, and stage-state communication. The updated version makes progression easier to scan, helps users understand what is completed versus unavailable, and creates a clearer relationship between current work and future locked stages.

Final Reflection

Social Reflection

The project began with the observation that free-riding is not only a productivity problem, but also a social and emotional one. Students may contribute unevenly for different reasons: some are unsure how to begin, while others lose motivation when effort feels invisible. SyncValley therefore aims to support both fairness and confidence by combining clearer structure with more encouraging feedback.

Ethical Reflection

A key ethical concern is how contribution becomes visible. If visibility is too harsh, it can easily become surveillance or public shaming. For that reason, the system avoids aggressive leaderboards and instead uses softer indicators such as task states, contribution snapshots, and forest growth. Another concern is AI evaluation: while it can structure feedback and reward distribution, it should not be treated as a perfect authority on human effort or collaboration quality.

Design Reflection

One of the most important lessons from the project was that our original game-oriented framing was less effective than a workflow-embedded approach. The concept became stronger when we reduced unnecessary RPG elements and focused on practical collaboration support. This shift helped the system feel more useful, more believable, and more aligned with the real needs of student teams.

Technical Reflection on AI Use

Rather than describing AI as a general assistant, we reflect on its use through two concrete examples from the project. In each case, we looked at the Prompt we gave, the Outcome the AI produced, and the Action we took afterwards. This helped us explain not only how AI supported the process, but also how its outputs were checked, revised, or sometimes rejected when they did not fully match the needs of the project.

Example 1 · Stage Workflow and Task Logic

Prompt: We asked the AI to help structure the logic for SyncValley’s stage-based workflow, including the rule that each member could claim only one task per stage, that work should be submitted for AI evaluation before being marked complete, and that rewards should only be assigned after successful evaluation.

Outcome: The AI generated a clear first draft of the interaction flow and helped organise the sequence between claiming, guiding, evaluating, and rewarding. This was useful for clarifying the system logic at an early stage. However, some generated suggestions simplified the completion process too much and risked making guided work appear equivalent to officially completed work.

Action: We manually revised the logic to preserve the distinction between in-progress guidance and formal completion. We also compared this structure with usability-testing feedback, especially the confusion around status clarity, and refined the system wording and interface states accordingly.

Example 2 · Portfolio Layout and CSS Refinement

Prompt: We asked the AI to help refine the portfolio section layout, especially the visual hierarchy of the Evaluation section, card grouping, colour consistency, and responsiveness across content-heavy blocks such as findings, reflections, and technical documentation.

Outcome: The AI provided useful CSS restructuring ideas and helped draft gradient-based card styling, spacing adjustments, and table wrappers for better readability. Some suggestions worked well in creating clearer grouping and softer visual variation. However, other outputs were overly generic or introduced styles that did not fully match the tone and structure of the existing portfolio.

Action: We selectively kept the useful parts, modified the CSS to fit the established visual system, and discarded suggestions that felt decorative but not meaningful. This process involved testing layout consistency across sections and manually adjusting spacing, backgrounds, and hierarchy until the portfolio felt coherent.
